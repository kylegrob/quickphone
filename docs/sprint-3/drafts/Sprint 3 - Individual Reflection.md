## 1. Conceptual Understanding Gained

I spent a lot of time working on diagrams this Sprint. The DFDs and ERD needed to be modified from our previous sprint, so it took time to go through what AI came back with regarding our project. I used to treat the two as separate drawings of the same shop, but the Sprint 2 feedback pointed out that the ERD had over twenty entities while the DFDs showed only a few data stores. Applying the rule that every ERD entity belongs to exactly one data store forced real decisions, and we cut the ERD from 22 to 16 entities grouped into seven stores. I also had to rename processes like Diagnose Device to Record Diagnosis, because a DFD shows what the information system does, not what staff do. I think AI can be used as a tool, but it's also important that a human can make sense of the output. There were times AI used shorthand or confusing names, so it took human intervention to make things readable and consistent. This matters for the rest of the software development lifecycle because the database will be built from these models, so any errors now could create additional work later. I'm hopeful that the up-front work we're doing with the clarifying documentation and diagrams will make the build phase smoother.

## 2. Decision, Tradeoff, or Challenge Analysis

The decision was how to acquire the system: a custom Django and PostgreSQL web app, a repair-shop subscription, or a shared spreadsheet. The custom build scored 415 out of 500 against 350 and 285 because it fits the owner's rules on purchase order approval, hiding customer contact details from technicians, and one view of both stores. The tradeoff is that someone has to support it after December 9, while a subscription costs about $3,400 to $3,600 a year for two stores, so the result reverses if nobody will maintain the custom build. I marked the result as provisional for that reason, and the scores are also our own weighted judgments. With less time I would lean toward the subscription, and I would have asked the owner about post-launch support much earlier.

### Alternative paragraph for section 2

The decision was whether a device can leave the shop before payment is recorded. The client's workflow ends with payment and closure, but nobody had said what happens when a customer wants the device first. We considered releasing only after payment, allowing a manager override, and allowing release with a balance owing. We chose release only after payment, with no override, because the client's main concern is devices going to the wrong person or leaving unpaid, and an override would be one more rule to design, test and audit. The tradeoff is that a legitimate case, like a regular customer or a card machine problem, now has no way through the system, so staff may work around it on paper and the records stop matching reality. This is a team decision and not yet client-confirmed, so if the owner wants an exception I would add a manager override that records who approved it and why, instead of removing the rule.

### Second alternative paragraph for section 2

The challenge was deciding whether drop-off appointments are required, optional or out of scope. Our Sprint 2 DFD started check-in with booking an appointment, and the professor asked whether appointments were required and where that functionality was modeled. We considered making them required, leaving scheduling out of scope, and making them optional. We chose optional, modeled as its own process (1.0 Schedule Appointment, FR01, UC-12), because walk-ins are a normal part of a repair shop and requiring a booking would add a step the client never asked for. The tradeoff is two ways into the system, so check-in has to work with or without a booking, and an appointment pre-fills the customer and technician but not the device, since the device is only known at check-in. That means some data is entered twice and the models needed extra care to stay consistent. If the owner wanted every customer to book, I would simplify check-in into one path, but I would still want to test that with real walk-in traffic first.

### Third alternative paragraph for section 2

The decision was how to record that a device went to the right person at release. The client's concern is devices being lost or handed to the wrong customer, so staff need to check ID, but storing ID details creates its own risk. We considered saving the ID number and type, saving only a record that an ID was checked, and not recording anything. We chose a checkbox that staff tick at release, and the ID number and type are never stored. The tradeoff is weaker evidence if a customer later disputes a release, because the system shows that a check happened but not what was checked. In return we hold no government ID data that would need extra protection, which is a real liability for a small shop with a student-built system. If the owner needed stronger proof, I would add the staff member's name and a timestamp to the release record before I would consider storing ID numbers.

### Fourth alternative paragraph for section 2

The challenge was what to do with device passcodes. The owner said a passcode is needed only for camera replacements, so collecting one for every repair would store sensitive data we rarely need. We considered always collecting it, never collecting it, and making it optional. We chose optional, stored securely and visible only to authorized staff. The tradeoff is that the system must know when a repair is a camera replacement, and we don't yet know when that is learned, or who counts as authorized staff, so both are open questions for the owner. If the owner wanted no passcodes held at all, I would drop the field and have technicians ask the customer in person.

### Fifth alternative paragraph for section 2

The decision was how to track parts across the two stores. The client said parts are deducted from stock automatically when a technician records them as used, with no reservation step. We considered one shared stock count, a reserve-then-use step, and separate counts for each store with no transfers. We chose separate counts per store, deducted on use, because it matches how the client described the work and keeps the inventory data simple. The tradeoff is that one store can run out while the other has spare parts, and the system won't help move them, so transfers would be a later change. Reserving parts would prevent double use, but it adds a step and tracking the client did not ask for. If transfers became important, I would add them as their own process instead of changing the stock model.

### Sixth alternative paragraph for section 2

The decision was whether the physical ERD should store values we could calculate. The logical ERD is in 3NF with no derived attributes, but in the physical design we kept repair_job.status, quote.total_amount and store_part_stock.quantity_on_hand as marked denormalizations. The alternatives were to calculate each value every time from history, quote lines and inventory transactions, or to store it. We chose to store them because staff look up job status and stock constantly, and reading one field is simpler and faster than recalculating. The tradeoff is that a stored value can drift from the records it comes from, so updates must change both together, and that needs to be tested. With a small data volume, I would revisit calculating them if we saw mismatches during testing.

### Seventh alternative paragraph for section 2

The challenge was how many processes to put on Level 0 of the DFD. Our model has nine, which is at the top of the textbook's seven to nine range, while Level 1 diagrams each stay at three to seven. The options were fewer, broader processes or splitting the work so each process maps to one clear job. We chose nine because the professor's feedback asked for processes that describe what the system does, such as Record Diagnosis and Send Pickup Notification, and combining them back into larger groups would hide that. The tradeoff is a crowded Level 0 that is hard to read on one slide, so we show it whole and in four parts. With more time I would test whether a reader can follow the whole diagram or only the parts.

## 3. Team Contribution and Professional Growth

One team behavior that helped this Sprint was letting Ehsanullah handle communication with the client directly. Having one point of contact meant we could discuss our questions internally before Ehsanullah took them to the client, which streamlined communication. It paid off when the owner's October 23 answers conflicted with an earlier requirement about Technicians sending email: we drew it as the owner described and logged it as an open question instead of quietly picking one. One area where the team's process could improve is Sprint planning. We typically divide the work and work individually, but the deliverables are connected, so something like a prototype that references old data store names or processes causes confusion and extra work. The Sprint 2 models disagreed for the same reason. A more formalized project management system with listed dependencies, and a walkthrough where each person explains a part they didn't build, would help. Unfortunately, several teammates tend to procrastinate, so that is a best-case scenario. My part this Sprint was to keep following up and pick up slack when things weren't getting done in time, and to check the documents against each other and keep the Change Log current so each revision showed its effect on later work. Delegating and trusting the team is still something I'm warming up to, so understanding people's strengths and weaknesses and assigning work they enjoy and will execute is a skill I'm still developing. I provided feedback where I could, asked questions, and tried to keep communication open. This was a challenging sprint, but adapting to a missed deadline or an unreliable teammate is a skill that will be useful in the future as well.
